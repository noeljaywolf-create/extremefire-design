Special-hazard protection

Clean-agent suppression needs enclosure and life-safety planning

Clean-agent systems are considered for selected enclosed hazards where water or residue could cause additional damage. They are not automatically safer or more suitable than sprinklers. Agent selection and system design depend on the protected hazard, room volume, leakage paths, ventilation, equipment and people who may be present. No single agent suits every space.

For a total-flooding design, the enclosure must retain the specified concentration for the design period. Doors, cable penetrations, raised floors, ceilings and ventilation openings can affect retention. The design must also coordinate detection, warning, release controls, shutdowns and safe evacuation. Occupants should follow the site alarm and evacuation procedure and never remain in a room because a system is described as “clean agent.”

Before planning an installation or upgrade, collect room drawings, dimensions, equipment and fuel hazards, ventilation details, occupancy patterns and existing cylinder records. Enclosure integrity and maintenance arrangements need to be considered as part of the complete system, not as optional extras.

Gas and clean-agent suppression

When the equipment is the asset, water is the wrong tool

The scenario. A server room holds live racks, uninterrupted processes and a replacement lead time measured in weeks. A conventional sprinkler over it is technically compliant in some readings and commercially severe in practice: the water stops the fire and starts a rebuild.

Clean-agent systems can suppress selected enclosed hazards without using water as the extinguishing medium, but they do not eliminate all fire damage or guarantee rapid re-entry. Their performance depends on correct agent selection, system design, enclosure integrity, detection and release sequence, and continued maintenance. Door seals, cable penetrations, ventilation and later room alterations can affect enclosure performance.

Practical lesson. Enclosure integrity, occupant warning and evacuation, release controls, maintenance and post-discharge procedures all form part of the design conversation. Re-entry should follow the site procedure and competent-person assessment.

  • Failure mode: Agent concentration lost through poor enclosure sealing
  • Primary levers: Agent selection, enclosure integrity
  • Also required: Hold-off, discharge detection and verification
  • Also consider: Enclosure integrity, warning, evacuation and maintenance arrangements

This is an illustrative industry scenario, not a record of work carried out for a named client. Testing and maintenance must follow the system design, manufacturer guidance and applicable requirements.
